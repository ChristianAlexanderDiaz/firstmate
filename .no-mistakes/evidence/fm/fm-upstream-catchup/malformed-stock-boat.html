  There was nobody on the landing. Then a kitchen door opened in one of the cottages above it, and a woman in a bathrobe came out holding a mug and
  stared. Then she waved, a big slow wave over her head, and kept waving until they were past.

  Next was Cobb's Wharf, at 7:45. Walt sounded the horn again. This time a man came out of the chandlery, and then the girl who worked the bait
  counter, and then two more people from the café, still in their aprons.

  By the time they reached the Narrows landing, word had gotten ahead of them. People phone each other on an island. Thirty or forty of them stood
  along the seawall, and somebody had brought a handbell from the primary school, and somebody else had a saucepan and a wooden spoon. When Walt
  sounded the signal, the whole seawall answered with a racket of clanging and cheering that carried right across the water.

  Teo stood in the wheelhouse door with his mouth open. "Did you plan this?"

  "No," Walt said, and found that he had to clear his throat twice to say it.

  Every landing was the same, and every crowd was bigger. At Church Steps the old minister rang the actual church bell, badly, out of time. At the
  school pier a whole class of children lined the rail in their yellow raincoats and shrieked the two-short-one-long signal back at them with their
  own voices. Some of them, Walt realized, had never ridden the Bluebell in their lives. Their parents had, though, and their grandparents.

  The last stop on the old route was the main island dock. Walt brought the tow around in a long, slow arc, and the Bluebell swung through it behind
  him as gracefully as she ever had under her own steam.

  June was on the dock.

  She was sitting in a folding chair someone had carried down for her, with a blanket on her knees and a thermos in her lap. She did not wave. She
  just lifted the thermos a little, the way you raise a glass, and Walt lifted his hand from the wheel in answer.

  They reached Harrow at a quarter to four instead of eleven, and the yard foreman complained about overtime. Walt paid it out of his own pocket.
  Before he unshackled the tow, he climbed aboard the Bluebell one last time, unbolted the green brass bell from her foredeck, and carried it back to
  the Hester in both arms.

  It hangs now on a post at the end of his garden, facing the water. On weekday mornings at 7:20, the neighbors say, you can hear it ring: two short,
  one long.
